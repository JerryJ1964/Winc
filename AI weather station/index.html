🌊 Gulf Stream Flow intensity · model temperatures · period

The Gulf Stream is a continuous ocean current, not a series of named yearly events. Flow intensity is measured by speed or water transport, not an earthquake-style magnitude. NOAA’s general reference gives an average speed of 6.4 km/h, a typical surface maximum of about 9 km/h, and about 1.6 km/h as the current widens northward. These are not annual measurements.

The list follows the globe’s warm-water path, including its North Atlantic Drift continuation. Temperatures come directly from the illustrative globe model, not observations. Year: not available — the model has no dated measurements. Local flow intensity is not supplied by the model and cannot be inferred from its temperature colours.

    Speed source: NOAA National Ocean Service (reference updated 2024; not an observation year). Temperatures: this app’s illustrative model, not a live feed or annual record.

    🌋 Active volcanoes NASA EONET · Smithsonian GVP

    The globe draws every volcano that NASA EONET lists as an open event — the eruptions and unrest the Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program is still tracking — as a cone that erupts lava: a glowing vent, lava bombs arcing out of the crater, molten streams running down the flanks and an ash plume drifting off the summit. The fresher the reported activity, the harder it erupts: a volcano reported this month gets the full fountain, one whose last report is years old only glows and fumes. Tap a volcano on the globe to fly into it.

    This list is not a forecast and not a warning — for official hazard information always follow your national volcano observatory. Sources: NASA EONET · Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program

    🔥 Forest fires NASA EONET · active incidents

    The globe plots active wildfire incidents from NASA EONET, newest first. It renders the newest 100 for performance and retains up to 250 normalized incidents in the feed. Each flame marks the incident’s latest reported position; when EONET supplies acreage, the list includes it too. The flame in the globe’s layer button glows only while at least one active fire is loaded. Tap a flame to fly to it.

    This is an EONET incident feed, not a forecast or a fire-warning service. Follow local authorities and evacuation notices. Source: NASA EONET wildfires

    🪨 Mudslides & landslides NASA EONET + modelled slope scan

    The globe shows mudslides and landslides in two clearly separated ways. Reported events (solid brown markers) come from NASA EONET, which logs landslide, mudslide, mudflow and debris-flow disasters worldwide — it is authoritative but sparse, so some months bring no new entry at all. Modelled slope risk (soft brown halos) is calculated from the keyless Open-Meteo model: rainfall of the last 24 and 72 hours plus the water content of the top soil layer, measured on a fixed list of known landslide-prone slopes. A halo only appears where rain has actually fallen on a slope, and it grows with the 72-hour total, the 24-hour burst and how saturated the ground already is — it is a rainfall-threshold estimate, not an official warning. Tap a brown marker to fly to that reported event.

    Modelled risk is this app’s own estimate and must never replace an official landslide warning. Sources: NASA EONET landslides · Open-Meteo (free, keyless)

    🏔️ Snow avalanches Avalanche.org + modelled snow-load scan

    Snow avalanches appear on the globe in two clearly separated ways. Official forecast zones are the live Avalanche.org zones published by the US avalanche centres (Alaska, the Rockies, the Sierra, the Cascades, New England…): each snow-ice marker is coloured by today’s published danger rating — Low Moderate Considerable High Extreme — and a zone with an avalanche warning in force pulses. Centres that are off-season publish “no rating”, which the panel reports instead of guessing. Modelled snow-load risk (soft white halos) is calculated from the keyless Open-Meteo model for mountain ranges worldwide: new snow of the last 72 hours, the strongest wind gust (loading), the modelled snow depth under the marker and any thaw with snow on the ground. Ranges without snow are left out, so the halos follow the real snow line — they are a snow-load estimate, not an official avalanche forecast. Tap an official marker to fly to that zone.

    Official coverage is the United States (the keyless Avalanche.org feed); everywhere else only the modelled snow-load estimate is shown. For any mountain day, read your national avalanche service — for the US: avalanche.org · model: Open-Meteo (free, keyless)

    🏜️ Saharan dust Open-Meteo air quality

    The 🏜️ tan haze on the globe is the Saharan Air Layer — mineral dust lifted off the Sahara and the Sahel by the harmattan, the shamal and the summer monsoon, drifting west over the Atlantic towards the Caribbean and the Amazon. It is drawn from the keyless Open-Meteo air-quality model (CAMS), sampled at 30 fixed cells across the dust belt: the source regions themselves (the Bodélé Depression in Chad, the Ténéré in Niger, the great ergs of Algeria and Libya) plus the Sahel and the two Atlantic outflow lanes. Each cell carries the modelled mineral-dust concentration in µg/m³ together with its PM10 / PM2.5 companion values and the aerosol optical depth (how thick the dusty column looks from space). A cell is only painted once it passes 30 µg/m³, so a clean day leaves the globe clean. Wind is fetched alongside it from the keyless Open-Meteo forecast model (the air-quality model carries no wind at all): the 🌪️ sandstorm swirls appear only where thick dust meets wind of at least 40 km/h — dust alone is a settled haze, and wind alone lifts nothing — and each swirl is stretched along the real wind bearing so it streams downwind. The haze is an estimate from a model, not a measurement or an official air-quality warning.

    Dust load is a CAMS model estimate and must never replace an official air-quality or visibility warning. Model: Open-Meteo air-quality API (free, keyless)

    ⭕ Earthquakes & 🌊 tsunamis USGS + NOAA live feeds

    The globe plots every USGS earthquake of M2.5+ from the past day as a circle whose size and colour follow the magnitude — the number in the circle is the magnitude, and events USGS flagged as possible tsunami sources get a dashed ring. Every tsunami message from the NOAA warning centres (NTWC Palmer, Alaska · PTWC Honolulu) is drawn as expanding ripples at the earthquake epicentre, labelled with its alert level (Warning / Advisory / Watch / Information) and — whenever the official bulletin reports one — the forecast and observed maximum tsunami wave height, with the affected sites listed under it.

    Smallest quakes (M2.5–4.0) only appear once you zoom the globe in, to keep the whole planet readable. Sources: USGS earthquake feeds · NOAA / NWS tsunami warning centres
